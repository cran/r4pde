Validation of standard area diagrams

Introduction

Standard area diagrams (SADs) are sets of images of diseased organs with known severity that raters consult while estimating severity by eye. A new SAD is usually validated by asking a group of raters to assess the same images without and then with the SAD, and comparing the accuracy of the two sets of estimates.

This vignette walks through the workflow of the paper that proposed the one-stage framework (Del Ponte et al., in preparation), step by step and with the same functions, using one of the five studies reanalysed there. Each section explains what a function does, what its output means and how it was used in the paper. The workflow has two parts:

sad_data()                            1. organise and check the estimates
   │
   ├── conventional, two stages
   │     sad_ccc()                    2. Lin's statistics per rater and condition
   │     sad_compare()                3. means and Tukey letters by condition
   │     sad_reliability()            4. ICC and OCC among raters
   │     sad_plots()                  5. exploratory figures
   │
   └── one stage, hierarchical model
         sad_fit()                    6. mixed model of all estimates
         sad_curve(), sad_regions()   7. bias, variation and expected error along severity
         sad_curve(type = "smooth")   8. functional comparison (GAMM)
         sad_raters(), sad_images()   9. rater calibration and image difficulty
         sad_fit(dispersion, family) 10. sensitivity analyses
         sad_simulate()              11. planning a new study

The example: soybean rust

SADRustSoybean comes from Franceschi et al. (2020). Forty raters estimated the severity of soybean rust on 50 leaves, first without and then with a SAD. Raters 1 to 20 used an old SAD and raters 21 to 40 a new one. The design therefore compares two SADs between groups of raters, each against its own unaided baseline. In the paper this is one of the two studies with two SADs.

1. Preparing the data: sad_data()

sad_data() puts the estimates in long format, one row per rater, image and condition, and checks them (missing values, estimates outside 0-100%, raters who did not assess every image). The estimates here are in wide format, one column per method, so wide names those columns and its names become the method labels. group says which SAD each rater used, so there are four conditions: UN_Old, UN_New, SAD_Old and SAD_New.

x <- sad_data(SADRustSoybean, rater = "rater", image = "leaf", actual = "actual",
              wide = c(UN = "unaided", SAD = "aided"), group = "sad")

Part 1. The conventional two-stage analysis

The conventional analysis first summarises each rater under each condition by one number per statistic (stage 1), then compares the means of these numbers between conditions (stage 2). The paper keeps this analysis because it is how SADs have been validated so far, and the one-stage results are read against it.

2. Lin’s concordance per rater: sad_ccc()

For each rater and condition, sad_ccc() computes Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient, \(\rho_c = r \cdot C_b\), and its components:

  • \(r\), Pearson’s correlation, measures precision;
  • \(C_b = 2 / (v + 1/v + u^2)\), the bias correction factor, measures accuracy and combines two shifts;
  • \(v = \sigma_Y / \sigma_T\), the scale shift (1 when the spread of the estimates matches the spread of the actual values);
  • \(u = (\mu_Y - \mu_T) / \sqrt{\sigma_Y \sigma_T}\), the location shift, negative when the rater underestimates.
cc <- sad_ccc(x)

3. Comparing conditions: sad_compare()

The second stage compares the means of each statistic between conditions. Because the same raters assessed the images without and with the SAD, the means are compared with a mixed model with rater as random intercept (lmerTest::lmer()), followed by Tukey’s test (emmeans). Means followed by the same letter do not differ, and “a” marks the highest mean. These are the soybean rows of Table 1 of the paper, and they match Table 2 of Franceschi et al. (2020).

cmp <- sad_compare(x, cc)
cmp
#> Lin's statistics by condition - linear mixed model, rater random 
#> Means followed by the same letter do not differ (tukey, alpha = 0.05).
#> 
#>  condition  N       u      v     Cb      r  rho_c
#>     UN_Old 20  0.21 a 1.23 a 0.90 b 0.89 b 0.80 b
#>     UN_New 20  0.10 a 1.19 a 0.91 b 0.88 b 0.80 b
#>    SAD_Old 20 -0.29 b 0.97 b 0.92 b 0.90 b 0.83 b
#>    SAD_New 20  0.00 a 1.00 b 1.00 a 0.97 a 0.96 a

Only the new SAD improved \(\rho_c\) significantly (0.96, against 0.83 with the old SAD and 0.80 without aid). The old SAD did change \(u\), which became negative, a sign of underestimation. The conventional analysis stops here: it cannot say whether that underestimation is constant or grows with severity.

4. Inter-rater reliability: sad_reliability()

sad_reliability() gives, for each condition, the two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-rater intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(A,1), McGraw and Wong 1996) and the overall concordance correlation coefficient (OCC, Barnhart et al. 2002).

sad_reliability(x)

5. Exploratory plots: sad_plots()

sad_plots() returns a list of ggplot objects: estimates against actual severity, boxplots of each Lin statistic, and the gain in \(\rho_c\) of each rater against the rater’s unaided \(\rho_c\).

p <- sad_plots(x, cc, baseline = "UN")
p$scatter

p$box_ccc

p$gain_vs_baseline

The new SAD improved agreement for every rater, while the old SAD helped some raters and made others worse.

Part 2. The one-stage hierarchical model

The two-stage analysis reduces 50 estimates per rater to one number per statistic. It cannot say at which severity a SAD helps or hurts, it mixes bias, calibration and precision in \(\rho_c\), and it ignores that some images are hard for every rater. Looking at the components \(r\), \(v\) and \(u\) does not solve this, because they are also one number per rater, averaged over the severity range. The one-stage model uses every estimate.

6. Fitting the model: sad_fit()

Let \(y_{ijc}\) be the estimate of rater \(j\) for image \(i\) under condition \(c\), and \(z_i = (a_i - 10)/10\) the actual severity \(a_i\), centred at 10% and in units of 10 percentage points (p.p.). The model is

\[y_{ijc} = (\beta_{0c} + b_{0jm}) + (\beta_{1c} + b_{1jm})\, z_i + v_i + w_{ic} + \varepsilon_{ijc}\]

  • \(\beta_{0c}\) and \(\beta_{1c}\) are the mean estimate at 10% severity and the calibration slope of condition \(c\) (a slope of 1 is perfect calibration);
  • \(b_{0jm}\) and \(b_{1jm}\) are the intercept and slope of rater \(j\) under method \(m\), so each rater can respond differently to the SAD;
  • \(v_i\) is the difficulty of image \(i\), shared by all raters;
  • \(w_{ic}\) is an image by condition effect;
  • \(\varepsilon_{ijc}\) is the residual error, whose variance changes with severity and condition (dispersion = "quad": log SD quadratic in \(z\)).

The rater effects matter most. The simulation in the paper showed that a simpler model, with the same rater effects with and without the SAD, attributes individual changes to the SAD and has a type I error near 35%.

Rich random-effects structures do not always converge, so structure = "auto" (the default) tries four nested structures in order and keeps the first with a positive definite Hessian and finite standard errors: "full" (as above), "noim" (without \(w_{ic}\)), "sep" (rater intercepts and slopes uncorrelated) and "base" (rater effects common to all methods). Under "base" the model cannot say whether the SAD changed the variation among raters, so report which structure was retained.

fit <- sad_fit(x)

7. Curves along the severity range: sad_curve() and sad_regions()

sad_curve() turns the fitted model into curves over a grid of severities within the observed range of each condition:

  • bias, \(\beta_{0c} + \beta_{1c} z - a\), with pointwise 95% confidence intervals;
  • sd_rater, the standard deviation among raters, from the covariance of rater intercepts and slopes of the method;
  • sd_image and sd_resid, the image and residual standard deviations;
  • expected_abs_error (EAE), the expected absolute error of one estimate made by a new rater on a new image. It combines bias and all variance components (the mean of a folded normal distribution) and is in percentage points, so it can be compared directly with the accuracy a user needs.

Differences between pairs of conditions are computed over the common severity range with 95% simultaneous confidence bands (from the maximum absolute \(t\) statistic over the grid), so a difference can be read anywhere along the curve without inflating the error rate.

reference = "UN" compares each SAD with the unaided estimates of the same raters. In the paper, pairs was used instead, to add the comparison of the two SADs (New minus Old):

pairs <- data.frame(a = c("SAD_Old", "SAD_New", "SAD_New"),
                    b = c("UN_Old",  "UN_New",  "SAD_Old"))
cv <- sad_curve(fit, pairs = pairs)

sad_regions() lists the severity ranges where each difference is significant, and its direction:

sad_regions(cv)

The plot method draws the bias, the EAE or the differences:

plot(cv, "bias")

plot(cv, "expected_abs_error")

plot(cv, "difference")

The curves tell a different story for each SAD. The old SAD shifted estimates downwards, and the shift grew with severity, from about -2 p.p. at 10% to about -7 p.p. at 50%. The new SAD did not change the bias significantly anywhere in the range; its benefit was precision, which shows in the EAE at 50% severity (about 4.5 p.p. with the new SAD, against 8 p.p. with the old one).

8. A functional model for the error curves: sad_curve(type = "smooth")

The linear model assumes a straight bias line in each condition. With type = "smooth", sad_curve() fits a generalised additive mixed model (GAMM, mgcv::bam()) to the errors \(y_{ijc} - a_i\): a smooth function of actual severity for each condition, a random smooth curve for each rater by condition, and a random image effect. Differences between conditions get simultaneous bands in the same way.

cs <- sad_curve(fit, type = "smooth", pairs = pairs)
sad_regions(cs)
#> # A tibble: 2 × 4
#>   contrast           from    to direction
#>   <chr>             <dbl> <dbl> <chr>    
#> 1 SAD_Old - UN_Old   18.0  84   a < b    
#> 2 SAD_New - SAD_Old  31.6  78.1 a > b
plot(cs, "difference")

In the paper, the linear model found the new SAD less negatively biased than the old one from 7% severity upwards, and the GAMM only from 31% upwards. When the two models disagree, report both and give more weight to effects found by both.

9. Raters and images: sad_raters(), plot_sad_raters(), sad_images()

sad_raters() returns, for each rater and condition, the predicted bias at 10% severity and the calibration slope (best linear unbiased predictors). The plot links the same rater across conditions; it is Figure 3 of the paper.

rt <- sad_raters(fit)
plot_sad_raters(rt)

With the new SAD, most raters moved close to no bias and to a slope of one; with the old SAD, many moved to underestimation with slopes below one.

sad_images() returns the difficulty of each image, the amount by which all raters tended to over- or underestimate it, sorted from the largest absolute value. Images at the top deserve a second look, for instance to check the actual severity measured by image analysis.

10. Sensitivity analyses

The paper checked two assumptions of the model with arguments of sad_fit().

Residual variance. Severity is bounded at 0 and 100%, so errors are necessarily smaller near the bounds. The default quadratic dispersion allows the residual variance to peak at intermediate severity. It can be compared with a log-linear dispersion, keeping the same random-effects structure, by AIC:

fit_lin <- tryCatch(
  sad_fit(x, structure = fit$structure, dispersion = "lin"),
  error = function(e) NULL
)
if (!is.null(fit_lin)) {
  AIC(fit$model, fit_lin$model)
} else {
  message("The log-linear dispersion model did not converge on this system.")
}
#>               df      AIC
#> fit$model     27 28896.59
#> fit_lin$model 23 29175.60

The quadratic dispersion fitted better in all five studies of the paper.

Bounded response. family = "ordbeta" refits the model as an ordered beta regression on the proportion scale, which accepts exact zeros and ones. The fixed effects are on the logit scale, so the check is whether the conclusions about the SAD effects stay the same, not whether the numbers match. The fit takes a few minutes, so it is not run here.

fit_ob <- sad_fit(x, family = "ordbeta", dispersion = "lin")
summary(fit_ob)

In the paper the ordered beta model supported the main conclusions: in soybean it shifted the mean downwards with the old SAD but not with the new one.

11. Planning a new study: sad_simulate()

How many raters a validation study needs depends on its question. To estimate mean agreement precisely and test its gain with the SAD, use the precision and power criteria for \(\rho_c\) of Romero-Benavides et al. (2026b), also available in their Shiny application. Severity-dependent effects need more information, and sad_simulate() checks whether a design is enough for them.

sad_simulate() generates paired studies (each rater assesses the same images without and then with the SAD) and returns, for each number of raters and images, the rejection rate at 5% of the paired \(t\) test on \(\rho_c\) and of the likelihood ratio test of the one-stage model. The SAD effect is set with shift (change in bias at 10% severity, in p.p.), slope_change (change in calibration slope) and precision (multiplier of the rater and residual SDs). The other parameters default to values typical of published studies. With shift = 0, slope_change = 0 and precision = 1, the rejection rate is the type I error.

Each scenario fits many models, so the calls below are not run. The first simulates a constant shift of -3 p.p.; the second a SAD that only changes calibration, two of the scenarios of the paper.

sad_simulate(n_rater = c(10, 20, 40), n_image = c(20, 40), nsim = 200,
             shift = -3, cores = 4)
sad_simulate(n_rater = c(10, 20, 40), n_image = c(20, 40), nsim = 200,
             shift = 0, slope_change = -0.10, cores = 4)

In the paper, the paired \(t\) test detected a precision effect well but detected a constant shift in only 16% of the studies and a calibration-only effect in 7%; the one-stage test reached 77% and 53% with 40 raters. With 10 raters its type I error was 0.13, so a parametric bootstrap of the likelihood ratio is advisable in small studies.

What to report

From the conventional analysis, the table of sad_compare() and the reliability coefficients, so that results can be compared with earlier studies. From the one-stage model, the random-effects structure retained, the bias curves with simultaneous bands and the regions where conditions differ, the EAE at a few severities of practical interest, and the rater plot. The code for all five studies of the paper is in its research compendium [GitHub repository to be added].
